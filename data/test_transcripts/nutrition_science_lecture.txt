## Topic: What Is Nutrition Science and Why It Matters
[00:00:05] Good morning everyone, let's get started. This module is an introduction to nutrition science, and I want to begin by setting up some vocabulary we'll be using all semester. Nutrition science is the study of how the food we eat is broken down, absorbed, and used by the body to sustain life, support growth, and maintain health. At its core, food provides two things: energy, measured in calories, and the raw materials the body needs to build and repair itself. Historically, nutrition science grew out of attempts to understand deficiency diseases - conditions like scurvy or rickets that occur when a specific nutrient is missing from the diet for a long period. Today the field has expanded well beyond deficiency to also study the opposite problem, overconsumption, and how diet relates to chronic conditions like heart disease, diabetes, and obesity. The central idea to hold onto for this whole course is that food is not just fuel; it's information and material that shapes how your body actually functions.

## Topic: Macronutrients - Carbohydrates, Protein, and Fat
[00:04:50] Let's talk about macronutrients, the nutrients we need in relatively large quantities and that provide the body with energy. There are three: carbohydrates, protein, and fat. Carbohydrates are the body's preferred quick energy source, broken down into glucose, which cells burn for fuel; they range from simple sugars, which are absorbed rapidly, to complex starches and fibre, which digest more slowly and have a gentler effect on blood sugar. Protein is built from amino acids and is essential for building and repairing tissue, producing enzymes and hormones, and supporting the immune system - unlike carbohydrates and fat, the body doesn't store much protein for later use, so we need a fairly steady dietary supply. Fat is the most energy-dense macronutrient, providing more than double the calories per gram of the other two, and it's essential for absorbing certain vitamins, building cell membranes, and producing hormones. Not all fats are equal though - we'll get into the difference between saturated, unsaturated, and trans fats, and why that distinction matters for cardiovascular health, later in the module.

## Topic: Micronutrients - Vitamins and Minerals
[00:12:15] Beyond the macronutrients, the body needs a whole range of micronutrients - vitamins and minerals - in much smaller quantities, but they're absolutely essential for the body to function properly. Vitamins are organic compounds and are generally split into two categories: fat-soluble vitamins, like A, D, E, and K, which the body can store in fatty tissue, and water-soluble vitamins, like vitamin C and the B vitamins, which aren't stored well and need more regular replenishment. Minerals, by contrast, are inorganic elements like calcium, iron, potassium, and zinc, each playing very specific roles - calcium for bone structure, iron for carrying oxygen in the blood, potassium for nerve and muscle function. Deficiencies in specific micronutrients cause well-documented diseases: vitamin C deficiency causes scurvy, vitamin D deficiency contributes to rickets in children, and iron deficiency causes a common form of anaemia. The key point here is that unlike macronutrients, you can't compensate for a missing micronutrient just by eating more food in general - you specifically need foods that contain that nutrient, which is why dietary variety matters so much.

## Topic: Food Preservation Techniques
[00:20:40] Let's shift now to a very practical topic: how humans have historically kept food from spoiling, because these techniques shape a huge amount of what we eat today. Drying, one of the oldest methods, removes the water that bacteria, yeast, and mould need to grow, which is why dried fruits, jerky, and grains can be stored for long periods. Salting and sugaring work on a similar principle, drawing water out of food through osmosis, which is why cured meats and jams last so much longer than their fresh equivalents. Refrigeration and freezing slow down the chemical and microbial processes that cause spoilage, though they don't stop them entirely, which is why even frozen food eventually degrades in quality. Canning involves heating food to destroy microorganisms and then sealing it in an airtight container to prevent recontamination, a technique developed in the early 1800s originally to feed Napoleon's armies. More modern techniques include pasteurisation, brief heating to kill pathogens without fully cooking the food, and vacuum sealing, which removes oxygen that many spoilage organisms need to survive.

## Topic: The Maillard Reaction and Cooking Chemistry
[00:29:05] Now for one of my favourite topics: the chemistry of cooking itself, and specifically the Maillard reaction, because it explains why cooked food often tastes so much better than raw food. The Maillard reaction is a chemical reaction between amino acids and reducing sugars that occurs when food is heated, typically above about 140 degrees Celsius. It's responsible for the browning and complex flavour development you get when you sear a steak, toast bread, or roast coffee beans - hundreds of different flavour and aroma compounds are produced, which is why browned food tastes so much more complex than the same food steamed or boiled. This is distinct from caramelisation, which is the browning of sugars alone without the involvement of amino acids, and happens at a different temperature range. Cooking also breaks down tough connective tissue in meat through the conversion of collagen into gelatin, which is why slow-cooked tough cuts become tender, while overcooking lean meat can dry it out because the proteins squeeze out moisture as they denature. Understanding this chemistry is genuinely useful, not just academic - it explains why techniques like searing before slow-roasting produce better flavour than either technique alone.

## Topic: Fermentation and the Role of Microorganisms
[00:37:30] Fermentation deserves its own discussion because it sits at this interesting intersection of food preservation, flavour development, and, we now know, health. Fermentation is a metabolic process where microorganisms - bacteria, yeast, or mould - break down sugars and other compounds in food, typically producing acids, gases, or alcohol as byproducts. Yeast fermentation of sugar produces the carbon dioxide that makes bread rise and the alcohol in beer and wine. Lactic acid fermentation, driven by particular bacteria, is responsible for yoghurt, sauerkraut, kimchi, and traditionally made pickles, and the acid produced both preserves the food and gives it that characteristic tangy flavour. Beyond flavour and preservation, there's growing scientific interest in fermented foods because many contain live beneficial bacteria, sometimes called probiotics, that may support gut health, though I'll say clearly that the specific health claims made about many commercial probiotic products often outrun what the current research can actually support, and that's an important distinction to hold onto as consumers.

## Topic: Food Safety and Foodborne Illness
[00:45:50] Let's talk about food safety, because this has extremely direct, practical consequences. Foodborne illness is typically caused by bacteria, viruses, or toxins contaminating food, and the most common culprits include Salmonella, E. coli, and Listeria, each with somewhat different sources and risk profiles. The core principles of food safety are often summarised as: clean, separate, cook, and chill. Clean means washing hands and surfaces to prevent contamination. Separate means avoiding cross-contamination, for example not using the same cutting board for raw chicken and salad vegetables without washing it in between. Cook means heating food to a temperature high enough to kill relevant pathogens, which varies by food type - poultry, for instance, needs to reach a higher internal temperature than beef. Chill means refrigerating perishable food promptly, because bacteria multiply extremely quickly in what's sometimes called the temperature danger zone, roughly between four and sixty degrees Celsius. Vulnerable populations - young children, elderly people, pregnant women, and anyone with a compromised immune system - are at significantly higher risk from foodborne illness, which is why food safety guidance is often more conservative for them specifically.

## Topic: Nutrition Across the Life Course
[00:54:20] Nutritional needs aren't static - they change substantially across a person's life, and understanding this is central to applied nutrition work. In infancy, breast milk or formula provides essentially complete nutrition for the first months of life, and the introduction of solid foods is a gradual, carefully staged process. Childhood and adolescence involve rapid growth, meaning higher relative needs for protein, calcium, and iron compared to body size. Adulthood generally involves more stable nutritional needs focused on maintenance and disease prevention, though needs shift again during pregnancy, when requirements for folate, iron, and overall calories increase substantially to support foetal development. In older age, calorie needs typically decrease somewhat as metabolism and activity slow, but the need for certain nutrients, particularly protein, calcium, and vitamin D for bone health, often stays the same or even increases, which creates a real practical challenge, because older adults need more nutrient density from fewer overall calories.

## Topic: Sustainable Food Systems
[01:02:45] To finish today, I want to zoom out from the individual to the system level, because food isn't just a personal health topic, it's an environmental and economic one too. Food production is a major driver of greenhouse gas emissions, land use change, and freshwater consumption globally, and different food groups have very different environmental footprints - producing a kilogram of beef, for instance, typically requires vastly more land and water, and generates far more emissions, than producing a kilogram of most plant-based protein sources. Food waste is another enormous issue - a substantial proportion of all food produced globally is lost or wasted somewhere between the farm and the plate, representing wasted resources at every stage of production. There's growing interest in more sustainable protein sources, including plant-based alternatives and, more experimentally, insect protein and lab-grown meat, though consumer acceptance and cost remain significant barriers to widespread adoption. As with the probiotics discussion earlier, I'd encourage you to look critically at specific sustainability claims made by food companies, since the underlying research is often more nuanced and contested than the marketing suggests.
