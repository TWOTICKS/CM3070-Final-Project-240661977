## Topic: The Founding and Early Kingdom of Rome
[00:00:05] Morning everyone, let's get started. Today we're beginning our survey of ancient Rome, and I want to start with the legendary founding, because it tells you a lot about how the Romans saw themselves. According to tradition, Rome was founded in 753 BCE by Romulus, who along with his twin brother Remus had been abandoned as infants and raised by a she-wolf. Romulus killed Remus in a dispute over where to build the city, became its first king, and gave Rome its name. Archaeologically, we know the truth is messier - there were scattered hilltop settlements on the site well before any single founding date, and they gradually merged into one city. For roughly two and a half centuries, Rome was ruled by a series of kings, seven according to tradition, ending with Tarquin the Proud, whose tyranny supposedly triggered a rebellion in 509 BCE that abolished the monarchy entirely. That event, real or partly mythologised, became the founding story the Romans told about their hatred of one-man rule, which matters a great deal for everything we'll discuss for the rest of the course.

## Topic: The Roman Republic and Its Institutions
[00:06:40] After the kings were expelled, Rome became a republic, and I want you to understand its structure because it's genuinely influential on modern government. Power was split between several institutions to prevent any one person accumulating too much of it. The Senate, made up of wealthy landowning aristocrats called patricians, advised on policy and controlled finances, though it technically had no formal lawmaking power. Executive authority was held by two consuls, elected annually, who each could veto the other, which was a deliberate check against tyranny. Below them were various other magistrates handling things like the treasury, the courts, and city administration. Ordinary citizens, called plebeians, had their own assembly and eventually won the right to elect officials called tribunes, who could veto actions by the Senate or magistrates that harmed plebeian interests. This ongoing struggle between patricians and plebeians, known as the Conflict of the Orders, gradually expanded political rights over roughly two hundred years, and it's a useful lens for understanding how the Republic evolved.

## Topic: The Punic Wars and Roman Expansion
[00:14:20] Rome's transformation from a regional power into a Mediterranean superpower really begins with the Punic Wars against Carthage, a rival trading city in North Africa. There were three of these wars across more than a century. The Second Punic War is the one everyone remembers, because of Hannibal, the Carthaginian general who famously marched an army, including war elephants, across the Alps into Italy and inflicted devastating defeats on Roman armies, most notably at the Battle of Cannae, where Rome lost tens of thousands of soldiers in a single day. Despite these disasters, Rome refused to surrender, rebuilt its forces, and eventually a general named Scipio took the war to Carthage itself, defeating Hannibal at the Battle of Zama in 202 BCE. The Third Punic War ended with Rome utterly destroying Carthage in 146 BCE, supposedly salting the earth so nothing would grow there, though historians debate whether that detail is literal or just a powerful later legend. By the end of these wars, Rome controlled the western Mediterranean and had the military machine and administrative experience to keep expanding.

## Topic: Julius Caesar and the Fall of the Republic
[00:22:55] Now we get to one of the most famous individuals in all of history: Julius Caesar. By the first century BCE, the Republic's institutions were straining under the pressure of a much larger empire, and ambitious generals increasingly used their armies as personal power bases rather than purely instruments of the state. Caesar built a formidable reputation conquering Gaul, then in 49 BCE crossed the Rubicon River with his army, an act that technically meant declaring war on the Republic itself, giving us the phrase "crossing the Rubicon" for any irreversible decision. He defeated his rivals in a civil war and was declared dictator, initially for a fixed term but eventually for life. This alarmed many senators who feared a return to monarchy, and on the Ides of March, the fifteenth of March, 44 BCE, a group of senators led by Brutus and Cassius assassinated him in the Senate house. Rather than restoring the Republic, though, his death triggered another round of civil wars, because that's genuinely what tends to happen when you remove a strongman without replacing the system that let him rise.

## Topic: Augustus and the Beginning of the Empire
[00:31:10] The civil wars following Caesar's assassination were eventually won by his adopted heir, Octavian, who defeated his rival Mark Antony and Antony's ally Cleopatra at the naval Battle of Actium in 31 BCE. Octavian was careful, unlike Caesar, not to appear to be seizing absolute power outright. In 27 BCE the Senate granted him the title Augustus, meaning roughly "the revered one," and he presented himself merely as "first citizen," or princeps, while in practice holding supreme military and political authority. This system is called the Principate, and it's essentially monarchy dressed in republican clothing, which is a distinction historians care about a lot. Augustus's reign inaugurated roughly two centuries of relative stability and prosperity across the empire known as the Pax Romana, the Roman Peace, during which trade, infrastructure, and Roman culture spread extensively across an empire that eventually stretched from Britain to the Middle East.

## Topic: Roman Engineering and Infrastructure
[00:39:45] Let's shift now to something more concrete: Roman engineering, because honestly it's one of the most underrated reasons the empire held together for so long. The Romans built an enormous road network, over 400,000 kilometres at its peak, allowing armies, officials, and goods to move quickly across the empire; many of these roads were built with such durable layered construction that sections still exist today. Roman aqueducts, some using precisely calculated gradients over enormous distances, brought fresh water into cities for public baths, fountains, and private homes, and Roman concrete, using volcanic ash as a key ingredient, was strong enough that some structures like the dome of the Pantheon remain standing two thousand years later, which modern engineers still study. They also developed sophisticated sewer systems, most famously the Cloaca Maxima in Rome itself. This combination of roads, water systems, and durable construction was as much a tool of political control and cultural unification as it was practical infrastructure - it made the empire feel, and actually function, as one connected system.

## Topic: Daily Life in Ancient Rome
[00:47:50] It's worth stepping back from politics and wars to talk about what everyday life actually looked like for ordinary Romans. Roman society was sharply stratified: at the top were the senatorial and equestrian elites, below them ordinary free citizens, then freedmen who had been formerly enslaved, and at the bottom a large enslaved population that the entire economy depended on. Most urban Romans lived in multi-story apartment blocks called insulae, often cramped and at real risk of fire or collapse, quite unlike the villas of the wealthy. Public entertainment was hugely important to social and political life - gladiatorial games, chariot races at venues like the Circus Maximus, and public bathing complexes were central social institutions, and politicians used lavish games to win popular favour, essentially early political campaigning. Diet for ordinary people centred on grain, usually made into bread or porridge, supplemented with olive oil, wine, and vegetables, with meat being a comparative luxury reserved mostly for the wealthy or for festival occasions.

## Topic: The Decline and Fall of the Western Empire
[00:56:30] Now to a question historians have argued about for literally centuries: why did the Western Roman Empire fall? There's no single cause, but several factors compounded each other. Economically, the empire faced chronic inflation, heavy taxation, and a shrinking tax base as trade routes became less secure. Politically, the third century saw near-constant civil wars and a rapid turnover of emperors, many assassinated by their own soldiers, which severely weakened central authority. Militarily, the empire faced sustained pressure from various groups along its northern and eastern frontiers, and increasingly relied on foreign mercenaries who had limited loyalty to Rome itself. The traditionally cited end point is 476 CE, when a Germanic military leader named Odoacer deposed the last Western emperor, Romulus Augustulus, though many historians now treat this as more of a symbolic milestone than a sudden collapse, since the transformation had been under way for generations. Crucially, the Eastern Roman Empire, centred on Constantinople, survived for another thousand years, so "the fall of Rome" really only describes the western half.

## Topic: The Legacy of Rome
[01:05:15] To close today, I want to talk about why any of this still matters. Roman law forms a direct ancestor of legal systems across much of Europe and, by extension, many former colonies, particularly around concepts like contracts, property rights, and legal procedure. The Latin language evolved into the Romance languages - French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Romanian - and heavily influenced English vocabulary, especially technical and formal terms. Roman architectural forms, the arch, the dome, the use of concrete, reappear constantly across later European and even American civic buildings, often quite deliberately as a way of borrowing Roman authority and prestige. And the Republic's institutional ideas, checks and balances, elected representatives, term limits, directly influenced the framers of several modern constitutions, including explicit references by the founders of the United States. So when we ask why we still study a civilization that fell over fifteen hundred years ago, part of the answer is simply that we're still living inside institutions and ideas it built.
